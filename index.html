Practice Areas

Estate planning

Wills, revocable and irrevocable trusts, and probate — structured to protect your family, your property, and the legacy you intend to leave.

Zachary Weitz, Esq. · California State Bar No. 335524 · Admitted to the U.S. Tax Court

Overview

The plan is the first act of counsel

An estate plan is the set of decisions you make while you can still make them: who receives what, who raises the children, who acts if you cannot — and how much of the estate survives the taxes and court costs that arrive when no plan is in place.

We build those decisions into wills, revocable and irrevocable trusts, and — where it is required — probate, with the tax and succession consequences worked out before the ink dries. And because the same firm administers trusts, defends them when challenged, and handles the tax matters that follow, the plan is drafted by an attorney who expects to answer for it.

What This Covers

Wills, trusts, and probate — and the decisions between them

Wills

The foundational document: who receives your property, who serves as executor, and who would raise minor children. A will works through probate — necessary, but only one of the tools.

Revocable trusts

The working center of most California plans. A funded revocable trust generally administers outside probate, stays private, and provides continuity if you become incapacitated.

Irrevocable trusts

Structures where control is deliberately placed out of reach — used where protection, tax positioning, or a beneficiary’s circumstances call for it, with the consequences stated plainly before you sign.

Probate

When an estate must pass through court — no plan, an unfunded plan, or contested papers — we guide executors and families through the California process from first filing to final distribution.

How an Engagement Works

It begins with what you own and who it’s for

Every plan starts with a confidential consultation: what you own, who it is for, and what you want it to do. We design the structure in plain terms, draft and execute the documents, and fund the trust so the plan works on paper and in fact. As families, assets, and tax law change, the plan is reviewed and adjusted — one attorney, accountable for the whole arc.

Common Questions

For most California homeowners, the trust becomes the working tool and the will the backstop. The consultation is where we match the tool to the estate — not the other way around.

California’s intestacy statutes decide. The state — not you — determines who receives your property, and a court decides who raises minor children. The estate still passes through probate, on the court’s calendar rather than your family’s. A plan exists precisely to replace those defaults with your own decisions.

After the events that change a plan’s assumptions — marriage, divorce, a birth or death, a property purchase or sale — and periodically even when life is quiet. Tax law moves as well: California’s Proposition 19 rewrote the property-tax treatment of parent–child transfers, and plans drafted before it often no longer do what their owners believe they do.

Titling your assets in the name of the trust — the house, the accounts, the business interests. A trust that holds nothing controls nothing: unfunded assets can still end up in probate regardless of how well the document is drafted. Execution and funding are treated here as one job, not two.

Trust administration

Steady guidance for trustees, fiduciaries, and beneficiaries through every duty, deadline, and decision the administration process brings.

Zachary Weitz, Esq. · California State Bar No. 335524 · Admitted to the U.S. Tax Court

Overview

Administration is where a plan proves itself

When a trust becomes active — usually at a death, sometimes at an incapacity — someone has to do the work the document describes: notify, account, pay what is owed, and distribute what remains. That someone is a fiduciary, and the duties are legal obligations, not suggestions.

We guide trustees, fiduciaries, and beneficiaries through every duty, deadline, and decision the process brings — the same firm that drafts plans and defends them in court, so the administration is run by counsel who know how these documents are read when they are challenged.

What This Covers

Every duty, deadline, and decision

Trustee guidance

What to do first, what can wait, and what must never be done early — counsel for trustees from the first notice to the final distribution.

Fiduciary duties

The obligations the law places on anyone holding property for another: loyalty, impartiality, record-keeping, and care. We help fiduciaries meet them — and show that they did.

Beneficiary counsel

Beneficiaries are entitled to information — notice, the terms that affect them, and accountings. We advise beneficiaries on their rights and how to exercise them.

Trust tax coordination

Administration has a tax calendar of its own. The trust’s filings are coordinated with the same counsel who knows the estate — no handoff, no re-learning the file.

How an Engagement Works

It begins with the documents

Bring the trust and every amendment, as they are. We map the duties and deadlines the document and California law impose, set the sequence — notices, inventory, accountings, distributions — and stay with the administration until it closes. If a dispute surfaces along the way, it is already in the hands of the firm that litigates them.

We represent individuals and businesses through audits and examinations, refund actions, penalty relief, and compliance matters at every level — administratively, on appeal, and, where the matter requires it, before the United States Tax Court, where Zachary Weitz is admitted to practice.

Refund actions

Where tax was paid that was not owed: a claim for refund, and — if the claim is denied — the action that follows it.

Penalty relief

Penalties are contestable. We build the reasonable-cause and compliance record that relief stands on, at audit and post-audit stages alike.

Compliance representation

Bringing filings current and keeping them there — the quiet end of most controversies, reached deliberately.

U.S. Tax Court

When administrative remedies are exhausted or the forum is right, the matter is litigated by counsel already admitted to the court.

Overview

Generosity, properly structured

Giving at any scale has a structure available to it — and the wrong structure quietly taxes generosity itself. Charitable planning fits the gift to the giver: what is given, when, and through what vehicle determines what the charity receives and what the donor’s estate keeps.

We form nonprofits and private foundations, advise on the charitable side of estate plans, and prepare the annual Form 990 and 990-PF filings that keep organizations in good standing — the machinery of giving, handled with the same care as the gifts.

What This Covers

From the first gift to the annual filings

Charitable planning

Gifts designed into an estate or lifetime plan — the right assets, in the right years, through the right structure, aligned with the rest of your plan.

Nonprofit formation

Organizations formed correctly at the outset: purpose, governance, and the filings that establish and maintain exempt status.

Private-foundation formation

Family and single-source foundations structured for control, continuity, and the grant-making the founders intend across generations.

Form 990 & 990-PF filings

The annual federal information returns — Form 990 for nonprofits, Form 990-PF for private foundations — prepared and filed accurately and on time.

How an Engagement Works

It begins with what the giving is for

Every engagement starts with the purpose: the cause, the scale, and the horizon. We recommend the structure that fits — a gift within an existing plan, a new organization, or a foundation — form it correctly, and set the filing calendar so compliance is routine rather than remembered. The firm that forms the organization files its returns.

Common Questions

What founders and donors ask

Directly, and often beneficially. Gifts can be made during life, at death through a will or trust, or through structures designed for both — and which assets are given matters, because some assets carry heavier tax to heirs than to charities. The annual information returns federal law requires of exempt organizations: Form 990 for nonprofits generally, Form 990-PF for private foundations. They are public documents — donors and regulators read them — so accuracy and timeliness are part of an organization’s standing, not back-office detail. We prepare and file both.

Yes — formation, the filings that establish exempt status, and the annual returns are handled here as one continuing engagement, coordinated with the estate plans of the families behind the organization.

Overview

The business, the property, and the plan behind both

For owners, the business, the buildings, and the family estate are not three subjects — they are one balance sheet. How an entity is formed, how a property is titled, and how both pass at death determine the liability, the taxes, and the succession more than any single transaction does.

We structure businesses and transactions, form entities, plan for real estate, and advise on California property tax — including Propositions 13 and 19 — with asset protection built into the structure from the start, coordinated with the estate plan the same firm drafts.

Lawful structuring, done in advance, that places what you have built beyond the reach of risks it was never meant to fund.

California property tax

Proposition 13’s protections and Proposition 19’s narrower transfer rules — planned for before property changes hands, when the planning still works.

How an Engagement Works

It begins with how things are held

We start with the current picture: what you own, how it is titled, and what the estate plan says about it. The structure is then aligned across all three — entity, property, and plan — before the next transaction, transfer, or signing makes the questions harder. One counsel reviews the whole balance sheet, because that is how the risks arrive.

Common Questions

What owners ask first

Prop 19 narrowed what Prop 13 long allowed. A parent–child transfer that once preserved the low base-year value now does so only in limited circumstances — principally where the child makes the home a primary residence — and value above a threshold is reassessed. The planning lesson is sequence: a transfer structured before the fact can preserve treatment that cannot be recovered after it.

The lawful arrangement of ownership, in advance, so that a business or personal claim cannot easily reach assets it was never meant to touch. It works when it is done early and for legitimate structure — entities, title, and planning put in place before any claim exists. Done late, or to defeat a known creditor, it does not work and creates new problems. Timing is the whole discipline.

There is no default answer worth having. The choice turns on how the business earns, who owns it, what liability it carries, and how it should pass at death or sale — which is why entity selection here is made alongside the estate plan, not off a menu. We recommend the vehicle, form it, and keep its paperwork honest.

Title is destiny. Property held personally, in an entity, or in a trust each passes differently, is taxed differently on transfer, and exposes the rest of the estate differently. We review how each property is held against the plan, fund what belongs in the trust, and plan the property-tax consequences before a transfer makes them permanent.
